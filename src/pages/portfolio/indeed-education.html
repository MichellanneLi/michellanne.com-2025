Background

I was on the Social Impact team at Indeed, and our remit was to advance the interests of job seekers without college degrees.

Through this work, we discovered that:

  • 62% of Americans do not have a college degree.
  • 47% of US job seekers on Indeed without a college degree have completed training programs, such as trade schools, boot camps, and online courses.
  • Employers value these training programs. They repeatedly described skilling programs as more than just proof of ability—they’re signals of a candidate’s mindset and personal qualities, such as: trainability, commitment to their field, and personal drive.
  • However, non-college skilling programs were underrepresented on Indeed resumes. In interviews with job seekers, they showed us how our education intake lacked the flexibility to accommodate frameworks outside of secondary and higher education.

Hypothesis

My team hypothesized that helping job seekers represent diverse skilling experiences would improve outcomes for those who don’t have college degrees.

Constraints

My team did not own the education intake surface. We therefore needed to collaborate with and obtain buy-in from the Profile team.

Furthermore, education qualifications are a core component of the Indeed Profile and essential to matching candidates to job openings. Therefore, there was a very high bar to making any changes.

I approached these challenges by exploring incremental changes and validating them via qualitative and quantitative data collection.

Starting with content

User testing new form copy

I started with the lowest LOE approach by asking: how much we could accomplish just by tweaking the UX content to address a wider range of education experiences?

I collaborated with the Profile team to explore the following changes:

  1. Swapped in new labels to replace “Level of education,” which was a point of confusion because it assumed a hiearchy of education that doesn't make sense outside of academia.
  2. Used alternative labels for “School” that spoke to more diverse institutions and settings.
  3. Similarly, tried out new labels to replace "School location."
  4. Added descriptive text with examples of the range of educational experiences to help job seekers understand that they could add non-college education.

After a round of user testing, I incorportated the feedback into the second draft. It was then ready for AB testing.

AB testing the form copy

My team was delighted to discover that the results of our AB test were overwhelmingly positive! The Profile team was convinced to adopt the proposed content changes.

Results of content updates

Improved data collection

The content changes led to an increased intake of non-college educational experiences, as evidenced by increased usage of the following attributes in the “Level of education” input:

  • Vocational Training: +18%
  • Online Course: +12%
  • Training Program: +18%

Better outcomes

Job seekers who used non-college education attributes saw an increase in positive connections and positive outcomes (compared to those who didn’t.)

Positive connections:

A collection of metrics focused on employer and job seeker engagement throughout the hiring process, including: invitations to apply, applications, and positive messages
  • Positive connections per job seeker: +38%
  • Percentage of job seekers with positive connections: +28%

Positive outcomes:

A collection of metrics on the outcomes of a job application, such as: hired status, interviews scheduled, and positive employer sentiment
  • Positive outcomes per job seeker: +26%
  • Percentage of job seekers with positive outcomes: +9.7%

Concept testing

Our success at improving education intake using UX content gave us the credibility to move forward with exploring more comprehensive design changes.

Tailoring the form

I began by developing three new concepts that tailored the education intake form specifically for training programs. Each concept included a variation of the following changes:

Collapsed “Level of Education” and “Field of Study” into a single input.

Abstracting a degree from its subject matter is very particular to academia (ex: “a master's in computer science.”) Our research showed that, for other types of education, a single phrase is the norm (ex: “coding bootcamp.”)

{{> project-image size='' style='' classes='' img='level-of-education-alt' alt='alternative inputs for level of education and field of study' caption=''}}

Labels that asked for information about training providers instead of broad labels that might speak to a greater variety of institutions. Information about the provider was considered important by both job seekers and employers as a signifier of the rigor, relevance, and anticipated skill development level of the program.

{{> project-image size='' style='' classes='' img='training-provider-alt' alt='input for training provider' caption=''}}

Ability to indicate completion. According to our research, the majority of employers and job seekers felt proof of completion indicated a standard of skill development and achievement.

{{> project-image size='' style='' classes='' img='completion' alt='checkbox to indicate completion and input for certificate of completion url' caption=''}}

Selecting type of education

Providing a version of the intake form specific to training programs necessitated creating a way for users to select the type of education they want to represent. Here are the 3 different approaches I designed and the results of user testing:

The forked path

For this concept, users were presented with 2 options: “Add secondary or higher education” or “Add training course or program.” If they chose the former, they were routed to the original education form. If they chose the latter, they were routed to the training program intake form.

  • In user testing, all job seekers preferred this design because the choice between adding “secondary or higher education” and “training course or program” was most easily understood.
  • When participants engaged with this concept, they were more likely to select the training program option.
  • However, the label “School or curriculum provider” was confusing. It didn’t feel appropriate for some types of training and prompted more precise responses than desired, such as a specific instructor instead of a training organization.
“I like this one here, how y'all have that one set up. So just go ahead on and add training programming. And at the bottom, we have examples. It's all just set up right there. It makes it a whole lot more attention grabbing, more, I mean, right there in your face.”
{{> project-image size='' style='' classes='has-shadow' img='Concept-A-1' alt='Forked path screen' caption='Forked path screen'}} {{> project-image size='' style='' classes='has-shadow' img='Concept-A-4' alt='Form tailored for training intake' caption='Training intake form'}}

The tabbed form

This concept featured tabs for “Degree-based” amd “Other learning.”

The tabs were confusing to some testers. “Degree-based” led some to assume they did not have the option to add their High School diplomas. “Other learning” appeared to be positioned as less valuable than degree programs and was too ambiguous.

“Other learnings, that kinda threw it off, I would say. I like the first one (“forked path”). It tells you what other learnings is. I mean, other learnings is a throw off.”
{{> project-image size='' style='' classes='has-shadow' img='Concept-B-3' alt='Form with tabs for degree-based education and other learning' caption='Tabbed form'}}

The toggle

This concept featured a toggle asking “Is this secondary or higher education?”

Most participants didn’t notice the toggle and believed that the form wasn’t meant to capture alternate skilling routes.

Quantifying job seeker interest

With such a strong signal in favor of the forked path concept, it was time to take a quantitative approach by opportunity sizing real world job seeker interest in the training program fork. Working with our team’s strategist, I created this user flow to facilitate a fake door test. You can see that, regardless of which fork the user chose, they would be taken to the same education intake screen.

{{> project-image size='' style='' classes='' img='fake-door-test' alt='fake door test flow' caption='Fake door test flow'}}

Test Analysis

Overall, results look sufficiently promising to move forward with the forked path approach!

In the test group, the “Add training program" fork accounted for roughly 1 in 5 selections.

Furthermore, job seekers were overall more likely to add non-college education attributes:

  • Online course: +25%
  • Bootcamp: +67%
  • Training program: +43%
  • Vocational training: -17%

Refining the solution

Seeing that the fake door test results indicated sufficient demand for a dedicated pathway for training representation, I wanted to revisit the UX content for the education intake form.

At this point, it had been determined that fundamentally changing the form inputs for non-college education was going to create too much downstream churn for other teams, so I focused on refining the labels of the existing fields. After another round of user testing, I finalized the content.

Rollout and Impact

Finally, it was time to fully roll out the new approach to collecting non-college education! It has been one of the biggest wins for the Social Impact team.

Results

Our redesigned education intake has resulted in:

  • +17%–171% collection of non-college education attributes
  • +2% job seekers with complete profiles
  • +1.9% positive outcomes for job seekers who add non-college education
